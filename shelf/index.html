Investing

  • The Intelligent Investor, Benjamin Graham, 1949. The book that founded value investing. Graham separates investing from speculating, introduces Mr. Market, the moody partner whose prices you can use but never have to obey, and insists on a margin of safety in every purchase. Read it for: the habit of never overpaying.
  • One Up on Wall Street, Peter Lynch, 1989. Lynch ran Fidelity's Magellan Fund. His argument: ordinary people often notice good companies early, in the shops and products around them, but only homework on the numbers turns that hunch into an investment. He sorts stocks into six types, from slow growers to turnarounds. Read it for: turning everyday observation into research.
  • Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits, Philip A. Fisher, 1958. The original growth-investing book. Fisher's "scuttlebutt" method means talking to customers, suppliers and competitors, and his fifteen points judge a business by its management, research and long-term growth rather than by a cheap price. Read it for: judging the quality of a business.
  • The Little Book of Common Sense Investing, John C. Bogle, 2007. Vanguard's founder makes the plain case for low-cost index funds. As a group, investors earn the market's return minus their costs, so the one edge everyone can have is keeping costs low. Read it for: seeing how fees quietly compound.
  • Mastering the Market Cycle, Howard Marks, 2018. Oaktree's co-founder on cycles in the economy, profits, credit and investor mood, and how sentiment swings too far both ways. The aim is not to predict the next move but to know roughly where in the cycle you stand. Read it for: thinking about risk when everyone feels safe.
  • The Psychology of Money, Morgan Housel, 2020. Short essays on how people really behave with money: luck and risk, the idea of "enough", why compounding needs time, and why staying in the game matters more than being brilliant. Read it for: the behaviour behind every money decision.

Trading & charts

  • How to Make Money in Stocks, William J. O'Neil, 1988. The founder of Investor's Business Daily studied the biggest winning stocks over decades and distilled their common traits into the CAN SLIM checklist, from current earnings to market direction. He is just as strict about cutting losses early. Read it for: a complete, rules-based approach to growth stocks.
  • Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard, Mark Minervini, 2013. Minervini, featured in Jack Schwager's Stock Market Wizards, lays out his SEPA approach: a trend template, earnings and catalysts, and the volatility contraction pattern for timing entries, all built around tight risk control. Read it for: how a momentum trader writes rules for entries and stops.
  • Think & Trade Like a Champion, Mark Minervini, 2017. The follow-up is about process more than picking: position sizing, when to sell, how to handle a losing streak and how to keep a routine. Less about finding the stock, more about managing the trade. Read it for: the discipline side of momentum trading.
  • Technical Analysis Explained, Martin J. Pring. A broad reference on trends, momentum, moving averages, volume, market cycles and links between markets, written for readers who want to understand why indicators behave the way they do, not just how to plot them. Read it for: a solid foundation in technical analysis.
  • Technical Analysis of Stock Trends, Robert D. Edwards & John Magee, 1948. The classic on chart patterns: head and shoulders, triangles, support and resistance, trendlines. Written in 1948, it is still the base most pattern books build on. Read it for: where chart-pattern ideas come from.
  • Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns, Thomas N. Bulkowski. Bulkowski measured how thousands of chart patterns actually behaved: how often they broke out the expected way, how far they ran and how often they failed. Read it for: testing chart folklore against statistics.
  • Profitable Trading with Renko Charts, Prashant Shah. An Indian author's guide to Renko charts, which draw a new brick only when price moves a fixed amount. Time and small noise drop out, so trend and structure are easier to see. Read it for: a cleaner way to read trend.
  • Trading the Markets the Point & Figure Way, Prashant Shah. Point & Figure charts record only price reversals of a set size, in columns of X's and O's, with no time axis. Shah explains how to build and read them, and what their patterns say about supply and demand. Read it for: a noise-free view of supply and demand.

Options

  • Option Volatility and Pricing, Sheldon Natenberg. The standard text for professional option traders: how pricing models work, what the Greeks measure, why volatility matters so much, and how spreads behave as conditions change. Read it for: understanding why an option costs what it costs.
  • Trading Option Greeks, Dan Passarelli. Delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho, explained through trading situations: how each one changes your position as price, time and volatility move. Read it for: managing an options position, not just opening one.
  • Positional Option Trading, Euan Sinclair, 2020. A modern, evidence-first book on where option edges really come from, mostly the volatility risk premium, and how to size and manage positions. Sceptical of many popular strategies. Read it for: an honest look at option selling.

Systems & risk

  • Trading Systems and Methods, Perry J. Kaufman. An encyclopedia of systematic trading: trend following, mean reversion, indicators, testing, optimisation and risk, with the formulas behind hundreds of methods. Read it for: the reference shelf of a systematic trader.
  • Introduction to Algo Trading, Kevin J. Davey. A practical path for retail traders into building, testing and running automated strategies, with a strong warning against over-optimising on past data. Read it for: a first, realistic step into algo trading.
  • Definitive Guide to Position Sizing, Van K. Tharp. Tharp argues that how much you risk on each trade decides most of your results, and uses R-multiples and expectancy to think about it clearly. Read it for: why size matters more than entry.

Mind & discipline

  • Trading in the Zone, Mark Douglas, 2000. Douglas says consistency comes from thinking in probabilities. Any single trade can go either way; an edge only shows over many trades, and only if you follow your rules every time. Read it for: accepting risk so you can follow your system.
  • The Disciplined Trader, Mark Douglas, 1990. Douglas's first book, on the mental habits that sabotage traders, and how to act on what the market is showing rather than on what you hope it will do. Read it for: the roots of trading discipline.
  • The New Trading for a Living, Alexander Elder, 2014. A psychiatrist turned trader covers mind, method and money: psychology, indicators, his Triple Screen system, the 2% and 6% risk rules, and keeping records. Read it for: a complete, balanced trading education.
  • Reminiscences of a Stock Operator, Edwin Lefèvre, 1923. A thinly fictionalised account of Jesse Livermore's trading life: big wins, ruinous losses, and lessons on patience, reading the tape and ignoring tips. Read it for: timeless trader behaviour, told as a story.
  • Market Wizards, Jack D. Schwager, 1989. Interviews with top traders across futures, currencies and stocks. Their methods differ wildly; their discipline about risk is the common thread. Read it for: seeing how many different ways there are to trade well.
  • You, Me and Trading, Prashant Shah. A conversational guide to the psychology of trading from an Indian author. Plain language, and more about the trader than the market. Read it for: the mental side, in plain words.
